Project 4 Web Page: Who's Doing the Thinking?

Who's Doing the Thinking? How generative AI can weaken or strengthen college students' critical thinking, and why the difference depends on how we use it

With a chatbot able to write an essay in minutes, what are students meant to be learning? A survey shows that 86% of college students currently utilise AI such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini for their studies (de Sam Lazaro). As I am a student, this is why I have decided to investigate this. As an accounting student at Rutgers Business School with aspirations to be a CPA, I will require the precise skill-set which people say is being negatively impacted by AI – analysing, identifying mistakes and justifying my work. There is much discussion around this issue. Professor of philosophy Megan Fritts has an AI free environment as she feels that by allowing students to access ChatGPT she has “lost the very best tool” (de Sam Lazaro). Conversely, some believe that students will still use AI and so universities should educate them on its appropriate usage. Moreover, there is debate about AI’s effect on students. Some research suggests that over-usage of AI is detrimental to students’ analytical skills. However, other research shows that AI can in fact enhance students’ learning if it is used in a thoughtful manner. I had initially assumed that AI would result in students becoming more lazy. However, my sources have shown me that it is not solely AI usage which is problematic. Students’ approaches to AI are key. Although generative AI does not inherently impede students’ analysis, it can do so if students cease to analyse their own work and begin to rely on AI to provide finished work. Thus, universities should not completely prohibit or naively allow AI. Instead, they should re-shape their teaching to firstly encourage students to analyse, and then use AI to further analyse, interrogate and build upon their analysis.

Background: AI Arrives on Campus

Students can now use generative AI such as ChatGPT to produce new material based on a question. This year’s college seniors are the first to have predominantly used such technology during their college career (de Sam Lazaro). Critical thinking is the ability to analyse, assess and synthesize information (Yuan et al.). Sarkar highlights that we are now in “the age of outsourced reason” whereby people are delegating this task to technology (Sarkar). Over-reliance on AI is recognised by researchers as problematic as it can cause students’ self-directed learning and critical thinking to be negatively impacted. There are also two differing approaches to thinking. Outcome orientation is concerned with the end result and process orientation is concerned with the learning process (Yuan et al.).

Perspective 1: When AI Does the Thinking, Students Stop Thinking

Students increasingly delegate their ability to question, analyse and assess to AI. Even high performing students can fail to recognise when they are doing this. Yuan, Liu and Yang discovered that the more students relied on AI, the less creative they became. Poor critical thinking was shown to be the reason for this. Furthermore, they emphasise that excessive AI use can cause students to ‘outsource’ their critical thinking skills. People who regularly fail to analyse and question AI responses will find that “creativity quietly diminishes” (Yuan et al.).

Other studies have also found that students that use AI more, find greater benefits from it but demonstrate poorer independent thought. They are also less likely to verify the accuracy of its responses (Benabbes et al.). Furthermore, a study of 398 undergraduates showed that those that commonly use AI have poorer skills at analysing and assessing. Again, top performing students were most affected by this (Galindo-Domínguez et al.). This is also seen in Advait Sarkar’s TED Talk. People who have greater faith in AI over themselves are less likely to think critically and act as ‘middle managers’ for their own minds (Sarkar). It is also evident within a classroom setting. When Fritts questions students she suspects have written certain papers, "a lot of times they can't" engage in a discussion regarding them (de Sam Lazaro). I concur with this view. Although these studies do not suggest that all AI usage is harmful, they do emphasise the risks of becoming too reliant on AI. Nonetheless, three different studies all involving college students have reached similar conclusions and so I am persuaded that there is indeed cause for concern in this area.

Perspective 2: The Problem Is Not AI, but How Students Use It

Whilst it is not the AI technology that is the issue, it is the way in which students engage with it. Students can benefit from moderate levels of engagement with AI and continue to develop their critical thinking. However, excessive engagement can begin to substitute for critical thinking (Benabbes et al). As shown in Figure 2, in Yuan’s third experiment, high levels of AI engagement reduced the critical thinking of students who took an outcome-orientated approach. Process-orientated students were unaffected. Similarly, those highly engaged with AI achieved a lower creativity score (2.99) when being outcome-orientated and a higher score (4.40) when being process-orientated. The authors state that "AI tools per se are not an inherent threat to creativity" (Yuan et al).

Sarkar demonstrates how this can be applied. His team's prototype allows a person to continue to analyse and form their own points. However, the AI provides opposing points and identifies errors within an argument. As Sarkar says, ‘AI should challenge, not obey’. Ohio State student Rachel Gervais also applies this method. She uses A.I. to create test questions as ‘I like to use A.I. just for a deeper understanding’ (de Sam Lazaro). I most relate to this approach. Unfortunately, the process employed in Yuan's research was based on a brief instruction and so may not last. Therefore, colleges must embed this process within their teaching.

Perspective 3: Ban It or Teach It? Where the Two Sides Meet

AI is difficult to monitor and the technology used to do so is inconsistent. Colleges should thus provide AI education and set students work which promotes independent thinking and which students can then utilise AI for. Some professors continue to advocate a ban and do so for important reasons. In an article for PBS NewsHour, Fritts describes how she enforces a zero tolerance policy and interviews students to assess their understanding of their work. Nevertheless, she claims that enforcing it "has sometimes made teaching feel like policing" (de Sam Lazaro). Alongside monitoring issues, there are also problems with the detectors. As one vice provost recognises, "the technology is outpacing our ability to detect it." Students such as Ashley Dunn from LSU have even been wrongly identified as using AI. Whilst Dunn was awarded an A, some people who viewed her video were given a mark of zero (de Sam Lazaro). Conversely, Ohio State promotes AI fluency. All undergraduate students there must receive education on AI and its uses. As Professor Lori Kendall states, "they're going to use it anyway," Provost Ravi Bellamkonda adds that the key issue is to establish "what can be off-loaded to technology" (de Sam Lazaro). Despite the apparently contrasting views, both sides agree that students should independently think. Yuan's team suggest "reflective integration rather than prohibition." For example, students can analyse three AI-generated thesis statements and then produce their own (Yuan et al). Similarly, Galindo-Domínguez and others advise providing students with AI education and work that promotes independent thought prior to AI use (Galindo-Domínguez et al). I support the blending method but agree with the banists that students should initially think for themselves. As an aspiring accountant I will need to use and identify errors in AI. A ban would therefore not adequately equip me. Instead of solely assessing students’ end products, colleges should evaluate their thinking.

Conclusion: A Tool That Makes You Think

This research has also altered my understanding of technology. Whilst students can still critically analyse, their critical analysis is eroded if they continually allow AI to do this for them. Most research has been carried out at a single point in time and/or within one university, so the long-term impacts remain unclear. Furthermore, it is still uncertain precisely where this ‘line’ is. Students can complete work and then submit it to AI which can both test and question them. Professors can assess both the process and output. Furthermore, colleges can educate students in how to appropriately utilise AI, rather than simply using detection programmes. Students can also be tracked throughout an entire semester. Sarkar concludes by posing a key question for all students to consider: ‘What would you rather have? A tool that thinks for you, or a tool that makes you think?’ (Sarkar).

Reflection

My general topic of interest was university students and their relationship with technology, for example, social media, sleep and screens. I decided to focus on AI and critical thinking as these are both relevant to my student experience and my future career as an accountant. Although initially I asked whether AI negatively impacts on someone’s critical thinking, I realised that my question needed to be more in-depth. Therefore I included a further element to my research question – at what stage does AI cease to aid someone’s critical thinking and then begin to substitute for it? This was the core message that I wanted to convey in my essay.

Throughout my research I learnt how to be discerning. I conducted research using Rutgers Libraries and free databases to locate peer-reviewed research. I discounted some sources as they were not relevant. A general article on issues with AI in education referred only perfunctorily to critical thinking. Further research was on the use of AI by young children to paint and so was not suitable for my topic. Finally, I identified a non-peer reviewed news article from a university source which provided a summary of research. However, I retained the three studies which analysed university students’ critical thinking as these allowed me to analyse data. For my videos, I substituted an interview discussing brain scanning with the PBS NewsHour video as it illustrated the differing points of view of actual university students and professors.

As my essay is for a public audience I aimed to write it in a journalistic, rather than scientific, style. I began my essay with a provoking question to engage my audience. I also explained key concepts such as ‘AI dependence’ and ‘thinking orientation’ in an accessible manner. Furthermore, I included subheadings to structure my essay and enable my audience to easily recognise the three different viewpoints. I converted data from research into language that a typical student would understand; for instance, I contrasted using AI to complete work and using it for self-testing.

Utilising a variety of media enabled me to effectively convey my viewpoint. The images illustrated information that would otherwise have been explained in a paragraph. Figure 1 demonstrates how AI dependence negatively influences a person’s creativity via their critical thinking. Figure 2 explains how a process mind-set is beneficial for students. Furthermore, Sarkar’s TED Talk enabled me to visually demonstrate effective AI utilisation via his live demonstration. The PBS video enhanced my essay by incorporating human elements. For instance, it featured a professor who felt she was ‘policing’ and a student who was wrongly flagged up by a detector. This created a sense of authenticity. Overall, alongside informing my audience, the media I used enabled people to visualise the decision they continually face when accessing a chatbot.
