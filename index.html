Who's Doing the Thinking?

How generative AI can weaken or strengthen college students' critical thinking, and why the difference depends on how we use it

Research question

Does AI weaken how college students think?

Does using generative AI tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude weaken college students' critical thinking, and at what point does AI stop supporting their reasoning and start replacing it?

According to a survey cited by PBS NewsHour, 86 percent of college students now use AI tools for schoolwork. As an accounting student who plans to become a CPA, I wanted to know whether these tools are making us sharper or slowly doing our thinking for us.

My thesis

Generative AI does not automatically weaken college students' critical thinking. It becomes harmful when students stop using it to question their own ideas and start depending on it for finished answers.

For that reason, colleges should neither ban AI nor accept it uncritically. They should redesign learning so that students think first and then use AI to challenge, test, and extend their reasoning.

Four terms that frame the debate

Critical thinking

Analyzing, evaluating, and integrating information instead of simply accepting it: questioning a claim, checking the evidence, deciding for yourself (Yuan et al.).

Outsourced thinking

Handing that work to a machine. Advait Sarkar calls this "the age of outsourced reason," where we "visit ideas" but "don't inhabit them."

AI dependence

Relying on AI so heavily that the ability to learn and think independently starts to suffer. Using AI often is not the same as depending on it.

Thinking orientation

The mindset a student brings to a task: an outcome orientation focuses on the final product, a process orientation on the learning along the way.

Perspective 1

When AI does the thinking, students stop thinking

All three of my scholarly sources warn that depending on AI for finished answers drains the questioning and checking that critical thinking needs. Across a survey and two experiments with 434 university students, Yuan, Liu, and Yang found that higher AI dependence went with lower creativity, and weaker critical thinking explained why. Over-reliance, they write, can quietly "outsource" critical thinking.

Benabbes and colleagues found that among 179 students, heavier AI users reported bigger learning gains but less independent thinking, and checked AI's answers less. Galindo-Domínguez and colleagues found that among 398 undergraduates, frequent AI use was linked to weaker analyzing and evaluating skills, especially among high achievers.

Sarkar's TED Talk shows the same pattern at work: people who trust AI more than themselves think less critically, and become "middle managers for our own thoughts."

Where I stand: I agree, with one limit. These studies show that dependence is dangerous, not that every use of AI is.

Perspective 2

The problem is not AI, but how students use it

Benabbes and colleagues describe a threshold effect: moderate AI use supports learning, but past a certain point heavy use begins to replace students' own reasoning. Galindo-Domínguez and colleagues add that students who treat AI as a complement hold up better than those who chase speed.

The chart at the top of this page shows the strongest evidence. When Yuan's team told students to focus on the learning process, heavy AI dependence no longer lowered their critical thinking. The authors conclude that "AI tools per se are not an inherent threat to creativity."

Sarkar's prototype shows what this looks like: the user still reads and builds her own argument while AI "provocations" offer counterarguments and point out fallacies. "AI should challenge, not obey."

Where I stand: I agree most strongly here. But the process mindset in Yuan's study came from a short prompt and may fade, so colleges have to build it into teaching.

Perspective 3

Ban it or teach it? Where the two sides meet

The case for a ban. Philosophy professor Megan Fritts keeps a strict no-AI policy. Reading ChatGPT instead of her students means she has "lost the very best tool" she has to judge her teaching, and many suspected students cannot explain what they wrote. Enforcing it, she says, "has sometimes made teaching feel like policing."

The problem with bans. Detectors are unreliable. A vice provost admits "the technology is outpacing our ability to detect it," and students like Ashley Dunn at LSU have been falsely accused.

The case for teaching AI. Ohio State requires all undergraduates to learn AI tools. Its provost says the task is to decide "what can be off-loaded to technology" and where people add value.

Where they overlap. Both sides want students doing their own thinking. Yuan's team recommends "reflective integration rather than prohibition," such as having students judge three AI-written thesis statements before writing their own.

Where I stand: with integration, but with the ban side's rule that students think first. Grade the thinking process, not just the finished product.
